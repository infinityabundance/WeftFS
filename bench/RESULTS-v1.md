times, and losses. It does **not** declare a winner, a record, a general

### 3.3 Codec analysis (scoped to this court and these corpora)

1. **Incompressible 4 MiB:** RAW is the smallest WeftCodec frame
   (4,194,404 bytes, 100 bytes of framing). ZSTD adds 105 bytes at level 3 and
   106 bytes at level 19 with no size benefit. External zstd is within a few
   hundred bytes of RAW but still larger. `auto` correctly selects RAW.
2. **Repetitive 4 MiB:** WeftCodec ZSTD level 3 produces 545 bytes and level 19
   produces 508 bytes. External zstd at level 3/9/19 produces 449/448/412
   bytes, xz 792, gzip 12,296, and brotli 104. Brotli is the smallest on this
   one corpus, at 3.57 ms encode versus 0.68 ms for WeftCodec ZSTD-3. This is a
   size/time tradeoff, not a overall result.
3. **Negative result — WeftCodec ZSTD level 9 on repetitive data:**
   level 9 produced 5,475 bytes, roughly 10× worse than level 3 (545) and level
   19 (508), while external zstd level 9 produced 448. The cause was not
   diagnosed in this pass. This is a real regression to investigate, not an
   outlier to hide.
4. **Near-duplicate 1 MiB (1% edits):** marginal DELTA is 79,984 bytes and
   marginal DICTIONARY is 24,733 bytes, versus 1,048,709 for ZSTD-3. But the
   required base/dictionary is another 1,048,576 bytes:
   - DELTA whole-pair = 1,128,560 bytes;
   - DICTIONARY whole-pair = 1,073,309 bytes;
   - ZSTD-3 alone = 1,048,709 bytes.
   Context conditioning therefore **does not reduce whole-pair bytes** when the
   base is counted and is not already retained. It only helps when the base or
   dictionary is shared across many objects and its cost is amortized. The
   receipt reports marginal and whole-object numbers separately for exactly
   this reason.
5. **Encoder cost:** WeftCodec DELTA encoding took 35.9 ms on the 1 MiB
   near-duplicate, versus 0.255 ms for ZSTD-3 and 1.77 ms for DICTIONARY. The
   current delta matcher is a bounded heuristic and is the slowest encoder here.
6. **Level 19 cost:** 95.5 ms to encode 4 MiB of incompressible data and
   661 ms for the 4 MiB source-text corpus, with size gains only on the latter.
   Level selection matters and level 19 is not free.
7. **`auto` behavior:** it selected RAW for random/empty/one-byte, ZSTD-3 for
   repetitive and source text, REFERENCE for the exact base, and DICTIONARY for
   the 1% near-duplicate. That matches the codec CLI's candidate rule.
8. **External process cost:** all external times include process spawn. They
   are not library-to-library comparisons against WeftCodec's in-process calls.

No `winner` field exists in the receipt. The scoped observations above are the
whole claim.

## 4. Store and context benchmark

Method: `examples/bench_store.rs` uses the real `WeftFS::store::Store` API. It
builds 100, 1,000, and 10,000 small files (32–256 bytes) with 1–3 relations
per object and three snapshots (`objects-only`, `half`, `final`). Fixed
timestamp `1767225600000000000` ns; `checkpoint_interval=4096`;
`gc_grace_secs=0` so garbage created after a checkpoint is reclaimable in the
same run. The store lives under `/work/.docker-cache/bench-store` (host bind
mount), not tmpfs.

Every query cardinality was asserted before sampling: 0-result tag query,
1-result one-hop relation query, n-result `kind == "file"` query, and an
`even`-tag query. The 10,000-object run samples `open(ReadOnly)` twice and
`verify(full)` once instead of five and three times, because each pass costs
~33 s and ~72 s. This is recorded in the receipt and is the only workload
reduction in the pass. The first full run used the larger sample counts and
took 797 s; it is retained as `bench-store-20261103T173129Z.json`.

### 4.2 Store analysis

1. **Ingest is fsync-bound per operation.** 10,000 objects plus 20,667
   relations took 173.4 s (~57.7 objects/s and ~5.3 ms per commit at the end).
   The store clones and re-encodes state on every commit and fsyncs the commit
   file, directory, and superblock. This is a durability/throughput tradeoff,
   not a free design.
2. **The 1,000-object ingest measurement is not stable.** The two final runs
   produced 13.0 s (76.6 objects/s) and 24.3 s (41.2 objects/s), an 86% wall
   difference. The 10,000-object ingest was stable across runs (177.3 s and
   173.4 s, −2.2%). A single 1,000-object throughput number should not be
   quoted; the range is the honest result.
3. **Replay and full verification dominate at 10,000 objects.** `open(ReadOnly)`
   took 32.6 s and `verify(full)` 70.3 s. Recovery validates every object's
   manifest at open; verification also reads every chunk. There is no
   incremental replay index in this version. For a 10,000-object store, open
   is three orders of magnitude slower than the 100-object case.
4. **Relation and tag queries are O(objects × relations).** A 0-result tag
   query and a one-hop relation query each took ~414–424 ms at 10,000 objects,
   because `evaluate.rs` scans `state.relations` for every object. The
   n-result `kind == "file"` query took 1.93 ms because it scans only objects.
   This is a major algorithmic loss: there is no predicate/relation index. It
   is not a measurement artifact.
5. **Snapshot and relation churn commits are cheap relative to replay.**
   Snapshot creation was 4.5–7.0 ms and relation add/delete churn 2.3–5.7 ms
   per commit.
6. **Garbage collection works and is expensive.** GC dry-run and apply each
   took ~51 s at 10,000 objects. It reclaimed the 40 unreferenced blobs
   (20 manifests + 20 chunks, 5,797 bytes) created after the last checkpoint.
   The dry run reported 40 candidates; apply deleted all 40. This is a
   correctness result; the time is a cost.
7. **On-disk bytes are reported, not scored.** After GC the 10,000-object
   store occupied 60,509,735 bytes (~6.05 KiB per object) including commits,
   checkpoints, snapshots, chunks, and manifests. That number is not a
   compression ratio and is not compared to any external store.
8. **No external SQLite or graph-store baseline was run.** The selector
   semantics (snapshot pinning, authorization filtering, evidence,
   `require_one` ambiguity) have no direct external equivalent without
   reimplementing the implementation under test. A SQLite or graph comparison
   in this pass would not be controlled. No competitive claim is made.

| tmpfs | rand-write-4k | 4,096,000.0 | 1,024,000.00 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.001 |
| tmpfs | seq-read-1m | 8,192,000.0 | 8,000.00 | 0.078 | 0.230 | 0.230 |
| tmpfs | seq-write-1m | 8,192,000.0 | 8,000.00 | 0.071 | 0.100 | 0.100 |

### 5.2 Filesystem analysis

1. **Rust vs Go, same court:** Rust measured higher throughput on the read
   workloads (random 4 KiB: 18,618 vs 10,503 IOPS; sequential 1 MiB:
   2,048,000 vs 630,153 KiB/s) and on the write workloads (sequential 1 MiB:
   20,791 vs 8,258 KiB/s; random 4 KiB: 73 vs 55 IOPS). `fsync`-per-write was
   effectively equal (127 vs 126 IOPS; p50 7.6 vs 7.8 ms). These are scoped
   observations for this kernel, image, mount options, and buffered fio
   workload, not a general ranking of the implementations.
2. **Writes are the dominant loss.** Every WeftFS write path materializes the
   whole file: `Store::write_file` reads the current content, rewrites it, and
   commits a new chunk set. On an 8 MiB file, a 1 MiB sequential write measured
   ~20 IOPS (Rust) and ~8 IOPS (Go), and a random 4 KiB write measured ~73 and
   ~55 IOPS. The per-write rewrite cost grows with file size. This is a real
   scalability limitation of the v1 store, not a FUSE artifact alone.
3. **`fsync` costs ~7.6–7.8 ms** per 4 KiB write at p50, consistent with a
   commit publication (commit file, directory, superblock) per write. The
   tmpfs reference has no comparable durability and is not a peer.
4. **Reads are warm and buffered.** The 8 MiB file was created immediately
   before the read workloads, so sequential reads are largely page-cache reads
   and the 2.0 GiB/s Rust figure is not a disk-throughput claim. No cold-cache
   run was performed.
5. **No direct-I/O numbers.** `--direct=0` was deliberate: same-inode
   concurrent direct I/O on kernel 7.3 can return `EIO` with this fuser version
   (`docs/fuse-status.md`). The benchmark does not hide that gap.
6. **No ext4/XFS/Btrfs/ZFS baseline.** The court does not provide those
   filesystems; the tmpfs directory is the only reference and is explicitly
   non-equivalent. No filesystem ranking is claimed.
7. **No metadata-storm or concurrency workload in this pass.** The fio matrix
   is single-job, single-threaded, psync. Create/unlink/rename storms,
   concurrent readers, and multi-writer stress remain unmeasured.

## 6. Stability

Each harness was run twice. The second receipts are the headline receipts
above; the first receipts are retained. `bench/scripts/compare_bench.py`
compares medians of the raw samples.

- **Codec:** 162 medians compared. Median absolute delta 1.6%, maximum 39.5%
  (external `zstd -19` on 4 MiB random: 130.3 ms then 181.7 ms). WeftCodec's
  largest delta was −11.6% (ZSTD-19 decode on random 4 MiB). Most WeftCodec
  medians moved by less than 3%.
- **Store:** 33 medians compared. Median absolute delta 0.8%, maximum 86.0%
  (1,000-object ingest wall: 13.0 s then 24.3 s). At 10,000 objects the
  ingest, open, verification, GC, and disk medians moved by at most 2.7%,
  except the 1-result one-hop query at −7.1%. The 1,000-object ingest
  variance is reported as a range above.
- **Filesystem:** 75 medians compared. Median absolute delta 1.1%, maximum
  18.3% (tmpfs sequential-write p50 at sub-millisecond scale). WeftFS deltas
  were under 7%. The io-bytes totals and direction of every workload were
  identical.

No sample was deleted. The two receipts remain in `bench/receipts/`.

## 7. Losses and tradeoffs (published)

- WeftCodec ZSTD level 9 is ~10× larger than level 3/19 on the repetitive
  4 MiB corpus (5,475 vs 545/508 bytes); cause not diagnosed.
- WeftCodec DELTA encoding is ~140× slower than ZSTD-3 on the 1 MiB
  near-duplicate (35.9 ms vs 0.255 ms).
- DELTA/DICTIONARY do not reduce whole-pair bytes when the base/dictionary is
  counted; they only win with a shared, retained base.
- WeftCodec `auto` on source text selects ZSTD-3 (762,372 bytes), while
  external zstd -19 reaches 573,917 bytes and brotli -9 649,512 bytes; the
  auto rule optimizes for one candidate rule, not for every level.
- Store replay is 32.6 s and full verification 70.3 s at 10,000 objects.
- Store relation/tag queries are ~414–424 ms at 10,000 objects and scale as
  objects × relations; the n-result kind query is 1.93 ms.
- Store GC is ~51 s at 10,000 objects.
- The 1,000-object ingest measurement varied by 86% between runs.
- Filesystem sequential and random writes are orders of magnitude below tmpfs,
  and the write path rewrites the whole file per write.
- Filesystem `fsync`-per-write is ~7.6 ms at p50.
- Go FUSE measured lower read/write throughput than Rust FUSE in this court
  (except `fsync`-per-write, which was close); this is not a general claim
  about the implementations.

## 8. Explicit gaps (not measured, not claimed)

- No external SQLite or graph/triple-store baseline for context queries. The
  selector semantics have no controlled direct equivalent; no competitive
  claim is made.
- No ext4, XFS, Btrfs, or ZFS baseline. The court does not provide them.
- No physical power-loss test. Crash evidence in this program remains
  process-fault proxies (`WEFT_CRASH_PHASE`).
- No energy or CPU-cycle measurement; no RAPL, no external power meter.
- No direct-I/O filesystem measurement due to the known kernel 7.3 EIO
  interaction; no cold-cache run; no multi-threaded or concurrent fio run.
- No metadata-storm workload (create/unlink/rename/mkdir/rmdir storms), no
  open-unlinked, hardlink, symlink, or xattr throughput run in this pass.
- No filesystem run at 8 MiB+ file sizes beyond the 8 MiB corpus, and no
  ranged-read or streaming codec benchmark.
- No Go/Rust codec performance comparison in a matched court in this pass; the
  codec numbers are Rust WeftCodec only plus external CLI baselines.
- No multi-host, NUMA, or non-x86 measurement. All numbers are single-host,
  single-kernel, quota-limited container observations.

## 9. Reproduction notes

- `WEFT_STORE_SIZES` selects store sizes; the default is `100,1000,10000`.
  `WEFT_FS_REPS` selects fio repetitions; the default is 3.
- Receipts are written by the court (uid 0 with `cap-drop=ALL`); the runner
  makes `bench/receipts` writable without widening source files.
- The first full store run (`bench-store-20261103T173129Z.json`) used five
  open samples and three full-verify samples at 10,000 objects and took 797 s.
  The two bounded runs (`bench-store-20261103T174517Z.json` and
  `bench-store-20261103T175920Z.json`) use two and one and completed in 489.6 s
  and 508.3 s. All three are retained so the reduction is auditable.

  rejects unknown court kinds and cannot disable the memory/swap/pids/CPU
