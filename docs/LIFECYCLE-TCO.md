### What the stitch cannot prove

- **It is not one end-to-end run.** The rows come from four courts and three
  fixtures: a Qwen2.5-0.5B checkpoint written by the store (write policy,
  crash proxy), a small CPU-only transformer in the Python court (async overlap,
  kill/resume), another small model for the partial restore, and a synthetic
  deterministic reuse trace for retention. Summing the rows would produce a
  number that no experiment produced, so no such sum is published.
- **Different runs, different dates.** The write-policy rows are from one
  revision and the async/resume/partial-restore rows from another; the receipts
  carry their own revisions. A change between them is a change in the code, not
  a measured lifecycle.
- **The retention rows are a trace, not a workload.** They show that class-aware
  ordering beats a fixed TTL on the *same* reachability-safe set, with zero
  unsafe reclaims in the class-aware policy and non-zero in the fixed-TTL
  policy. They say nothing about a real serving hit-rate.
- **The partial-restore rows are layer-scoped.** The receipt says why: a
  transformer's next training step needs every tensor, so "first useful step" is
  embedding + `blocks.0` forward/backward, not whole-model progress.
- **Nothing here is a GPU, a fleet, a network or a media result.** Every court in
  the stitch is CPU-only, single-host, and the crash rows are process faults.

## T089 — sensitivity of cost to price, given the measured penalty

### The measured quantities being multiplied

Both arms come from **one** interleaved court on one backend
([`augmentation-local-20261114T195000Z.json`](receipts/augmentation-local-20261114T195000Z.json),
full 101-tensor configuration, seven repetitions), so the delta is the
augmentation's delta and nothing else. A is the backend's own per-tensor durable
write; C is the shipped WeftFS store with the shipped planned representation.

<!-- BEGIN GENERATED:sensitivity-base (scripts/perf_numbers.py) -->
A's physical footprint is **424,439,296 B** and C's is
**282,106,049 B**: **33.5 %** fewer bytes, i.e.
**142,333,247 B** saved out of a **424,439,296 B** logical
checkpoint. C costs **970.0 ms** more write CPU and
**1,090.0 ms** more restore CPU per checkpoint, and in wall
time **832.4 ms** more to write and
**768.2 ms** more to restore, because it is CPU-bound where
A waits on storage.

The measured totals behind those deltas: A write CPU **110.0 ms**,
restore CPU **190.0 ms**, write wall
**339.3 ms**, cold restore **626.5 ms**;
C write CPU **1,080.0 ms**, restore CPU
**1,280.0 ms**, write wall **1,171.7 ms**,
cold restore **1,394.7 ms**. **7** repetitions
each, interleaved.
<!-- END GENERATED:sensitivity-base -->

Two billing interpretations are carried, because they disagree in principle and
a reader should see both:

- **`cpu_seconds`** — the CPU the process actually consumed (user+sys), as
  measured in the receipts.
- **`allocated_vcpu_seconds`** — the wall time the process occupies its vCPU,
  including `fsync` waits. This is what a provider that reserves CPU bills and it
  is the less favourable model for the arm that waits on storage.

### Break-even surface

Fixed geography of the model: one checkpoint per day, steady state, so
`retention_days` checkpoints are retained on average; `restores_per_checkpoint`
restores inside the retention window; storage is priced per GiB-month of
retained physical footprint and compute per vCPU-hour of
(write + restores × restore). All of that is an assumption about the workload
and is published with the result.

For each cell, C costs
`delta = -p_storage × saved_GiB_months + p_compute × extra_vCPU_hours`. C is
cheaper exactly when `p_storage > p_compute × extra / saved` and equivalently
when `p_compute < p_storage × saved / extra`. Both break-evens below hold the
other price at its cited value, so they can be read directly.

The compute axis is the one that can flip it. At seven days' retention with no restore in
the window, the break-even compute price is **68.876** USD/vCPU-hour;
with ten restores inside the window it falls to **5.628**. That is
the price at which the extra decode work would exactly consume the byte saving. At one
day and ten restores it is **0.804**; at thirty days and ten restores
**24.122**.

The storage axis barely bites in the other direction: at seven days and one restore the
storage price would have to fall to **0.000025** USD/GiB-month before
the native arm wins. The C-minus-A difference in that cell is
**-0.018535** USD per checkpoint lifecycle under `cpu_seconds`.

### What is deliberately not priced

| Quantity | Why it is not priced |
|---|---|
| request count | The with/without court is POSIX, so its "requests" are file operations. Pricing them at a per-1,000-request object-store rate would compare different units. |
| wall-clock seconds | A wall second of one process on four shared vCPUs is not a billable unit. `allocated_vcpu_seconds` is the modelled version of that idea, and it is a separate, labelled billing interpretation rather than a claim. |
| bytes over a network | The measured court is local; no egress or transfer quantity exists to price. |
| peak RSS | No memory-reservation price is cited. The difference is published as a resource quantity and as a loss, not as money. |
| engineering, operations, GC labour | Not measured by any receipt, so not priced. |

### What this is not

- **Not a forecast.** No growth, no fleet, no utilisation ramp, no commitment
  discount, no spot pricing, no reserved instances, no negotiated rate.
- **Not a claim that the codec is cheaper in practice.** It is a statement that
  *at these cited list prices and this measured delta*, the byte saving is
  larger than the measured compute penalty — including where that flips, which
  is the point of a break-even.
- **Not a comparison against another filesystem.** A and C are the same backend
  with and without this augmentation (rule 13).
- **One fixture, one host, warm caches, contended host.** The medians are
  inflated by a shared host; the sensitivity inherits that. A different fixture
  with a different compression ratio moves the byte saving, which is the
  dominant term, and would move every number here.
